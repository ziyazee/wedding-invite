# Cultural Direction — Muslim Couple (Indian Muslim Nikah)

## 1. The Nikah Ceremony & Wedding Traditions

### The Nikah: Core Ceremony

The Nikah is the Islamic marriage contract — a solemn yet joyful covenant between two individuals, witnessed before God (Allah). Unlike Hindu ceremonies which center on fire rituals, the Nikah is fundamentally a contractual agreement solemnized with prayer.

**Key elements of the Nikah ceremony:**

- **Bismillah opening**: Every significant Muslim occasion begins with "Bismillahir Rahmanir Rahim" (In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful). This is the spiritual anchor of the entire event.
- **Khutbah-e-Nikah**: A sermon delivered by the Qazi/Maulvi (Islamic officiant) before the contract. It includes Quranic verses and Hadiths about the sanctity of marriage. The sermon typically draws from Surah An-Nisa (Chapter 4), Surah Ar-Rum (30:21 — "And among His signs is that He created for you mates from among yourselves, that you may dwell in tranquility with them, and He has put love and mercy between your hearts"), and Surah Al-Furqan.
- **Ijab and Qubool (Proposal and Acceptance)**: The central legal act. The bride's representative (Wali — typically her father) proposes the marriage; the groom accepts by saying "Qubool hai" (I accept) three times. The bride also gives her consent, sometimes through witnesses who visit her separately in a traditional purdah setting. This exchange of "Qubool hai" is the most iconic moment of a Nikah.
- **Mehr (Mahr/Dower)**: A mandatory gift from the groom to the bride, agreed upon before the ceremony. It is the bride's right and her personal property. The Mehr is announced during the ceremony and recorded in the Nikahnama (marriage contract). It symbolizes the groom's commitment and the bride's financial independence.
- **Nikahnama (Marriage Contract)**: The written marriage deed, signed by both parties, the Wali, and two male witnesses (or one male and two female, per Hanafi school). The Nikahnama details the Mehr, conditions, and rights.
- **Witnesses (Gawah)**: At least two adult Muslim witnesses must be present for the Nikah to be valid. They attest that the Ijab-Qubool occurred with free consent.
- **Dua (Supplication)**: The ceremony concludes with a collective prayer (dua) for the couple's happiness, harmony, and blessed union. Hands are raised, palms upward, in supplication.
- **Date distribution**: Dried dates (khajoor) are often distributed after the Nikah as a Sunnah practice, symbolizing sweetness in the new union.

**Who participates:**
- **Qazi/Maulvi**: The Islamic cleric who officiates and recites the Khutbah
- **Wali**: The bride's guardian (usually her father) who gives consent on her behalf
- **Gawah (witnesses)**: Minimum two, who sign the Nikahnama
- **Both families**: Elders and relatives attend; seating is often gender-separated in traditional settings
- **The couple**: May be in the same room or in separate rooms during traditional Nikah (the Qazi goes between them)

### Pre-Wedding Events

Indian Muslim weddings, while rooted in Islamic tradition, are deeply influenced by the broader South Asian cultural landscape. The pre-wedding events blend Islamic customs with regional Indian traditions.

**1. Mangni / Nishaan (Engagement)**
- Formal engagement ceremony where rings are exchanged
- Families meet and set the wedding date
- Sweets (mithai) are distributed to announce the match
- Often includes a small gathering with family elders

**2. Mehendi (Henna Night)**
- One of the most vibrant pre-wedding celebrations
- The bride's hands and feet are decorated with intricate henna patterns
- Female relatives and friends gather for singing, dancing, and celebration
- Traditional mehendi songs (mehendi ke geet) are sung
- The groom's name is often hidden within the bride's mehendi designs — he must find it on the wedding night (shared tradition with Hindu weddings)
- Color association: green (henna paste), warm orange-brown (henna stain)
- In many Indian Muslim communities, the mehendi night is an all-female event with dholak (hand drum) music and folk songs

**3. Haldi / Ubtan (Turmeric Ceremony)**
- Adopted from broader Indian tradition; common in Indian Muslim weddings though not an Islamic requirement
- Turmeric paste (haldi) mixed with sandalwood, rosewater, and milk is applied to the bride and groom (in separate gatherings)
- Believed to bless and beautify the couple before the wedding
- Color association: bright yellow/saffron
- Not universally practiced in all Muslim communities — some consider it a Hindu custom and omit it; needs verification by community

**4. Sangeet (Musical Night)**
- Dance and music celebrations, typically held separately for the bride's and groom's sides or jointly
- Family members prepare choreographed performances
- Bollywood songs, Qawwali, and folk music feature prominently
- This is a cultural (not religious) event, widely embraced in Indian Muslim weddings
- In more traditional families, this may be a women-only gathering with dholak and folk songs rather than a co-ed Bollywood-style party

**5. Sehra Bandi (Groom's Turban Ceremony)**
- The groom's mother or sister ties his turban (sehra/pagri) and attaches the sehra (face veil of flower or bead strings)
- She applies surma (kohl) to his eyes — traditional beautification
- A talisman (taweez) may be tied to his arm for protection
- Close family gathers to prepare and bless the groom before the baraat

**6. Baraat (Groom's Procession)**
- The groom arrives at the wedding venue in a decorated procession
- Traditionally on a horse (ghodi) or in a decorated car
- Accompanied by family, friends, a band (brass band or DJ), and fireworks/sparklers
- Music: shehnai, dhol, and Bollywood songs
- The baraat is received by the bride's family at the venue entrance
- The bride's mother performs an aarti-like welcome (varies by community — some Muslim families skip the aarti and do a garland/rose petal welcome instead)

**7. Jaimala / Garland Exchange**
- The bride and groom exchange flower garlands (haar) as a sign of mutual acceptance
- This is a cultural tradition more than an Islamic one, but widely practiced in Indian Muslim weddings
- Often done on a raised stage with playful family involvement

### Post-Wedding Events

**1. Walima (Reception Feast)**
- The most important post-wedding event in Islam — a Sunnah of the Prophet (PBUH)
- A grand feast hosted by the groom's family, typically the day after the Nikah
- All relatives, friends, and community members are invited
- The couple is formally presented as married to the wider community
- Lavish food is a hallmark — biryani, kebabs, korma, sheer khurma, and elaborate desserts
- The Walima is considered so important that the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) instructed even a modest feast for those with limited means

**2. Rukhsati / Vidaai (Bride's Departure)**
- The emotional farewell when the bride leaves her parental home
- She is escorted out by her brothers or male relatives
- Quran is held over her head as she walks out (a blessing for her journey)
- She may throw handfuls of rice behind her (shared Indian tradition)
- Family recites prayers and duas for her well-being
- One of the most emotionally intense moments of the wedding

**3. Chauthi / Pag Phere**
- The bride's first visit back to her parents' home, typically on the fourth day after the wedding
- She is welcomed with sweets and gifts
- In some communities, this is when the bride and groom visit her parents' home together

### Common Event Sequence (Typical Indian Muslim Wedding)

| Order | Event | Urdu/Hindi Name | Typical Duration |
|-------|-------|-----------------|------------------|
| 1 | Engagement | Mangni | 1 evening |
| 2 | Henna night | Mehendi | 1 evening |
| 3 | Turmeric ceremony | Haldi / Ubtan | Morning/afternoon |

## 2. Visual & Decorative Traditions

### Islamic Geometric Patterns

Islamic art has a deeply sophisticated tradition of geometric pattern-making, born from the emphasis on aniconism (avoidance of figurative representation in sacred contexts). These patterns represent the infinite nature of creation.

**Key pattern types:**

- **Star patterns**: 6-point, 8-point, 10-point, and 12-point star tessellations. The 8-point star (Rub el Hizb) is among the most recognizable. These interlocking stars create mesmerizing fields of geometric harmony.
- **Girih patterns**: Interlocking polygon tiles (decagons, pentagons, hexagons, bowties) that create complex, quasi-crystalline patterns. Found extensively in Mughal architecture.
- **Arabesque / Islimi**: Flowing, curvilinear vine and leaf scrollwork. Unlike geometric patterns, arabesques are organic — undulating stems, split leaves (palmettes), and spiraling tendrils. They represent the life force and growth. Often combined with geometric frameworks.
- **Jali / Lattice / Mashrabiya**: Perforated screens with geometric or interlocking patterns. Served as privacy screens and ventilation in Mughal architecture. The interplay of solid and void creates beautiful light-and-shadow effects. Common motifs: hexagonal lattice, star lattice, floral lattice.
- **Muqarnas / Honeycomb vaulting**: Three-dimensional decorative niche structures, typically in dome interiors and arch transitions. Too complex for 2D but the concentric layered profile can inspire section frames.
- **Shamsa / Rosette**: Circular medallion patterns radiating from a central point, like a sunburst. Used as central decorative elements in manuscripts and architectural decoration.
- **Medallion patterns**: Large central medallions flanked by quarter-medallions in corners — the classic Persian/Mughal carpet layout. Translates well to invitation card framing.

**For the template**: Star tessellations, jali screens, and arabesque scrollwork are the most directly usable patterns. They work as background textures (at low opacity), section borders, dividers, and framing elements. The medallion layout (central medallion + corner quarter-medallions) is a natural fit for invitation cards.

### Mughal Decorative Arts

The Mughal dynasty (1526-1857) created a distinctive Indo-Islamic aesthetic that defines Indian Muslim visual culture.

**Pietra dura (Parchin kari)**:
- Inlay work using semi-precious stones (lapis lazuli, carnelian, jasper, malachite, mother of pearl) set into marble
- The Taj Mahal's surface decoration is the finest example
- Motifs: highly naturalistic floral sprays — lily, iris, tulip, poppy, narcissus, and lotus
- Color: stone colors against white marble — reds, blues, greens, yellows, and black outlines
- **For the template**: The delicate floral sprays of pietra dura translate beautifully to SVG decorative elements. The white-marble-with-colored-inlay aesthetic is distinctive.

**Mughal floral motifs**:
- More naturalistic than purely geometric Islamic art — Mughal artists incorporated botanical accuracy
- Characteristic "vase of flowers" motif: an ornamental vase overflowing with arranged flowers
- Individual flower types: tulips, irises, poppies, narcissus, rose (gulab), lotus, jasmine sprays
- Floral borders with continuous vine scrolls (bel) containing alternating flower types
- Cypress tree motifs — the tall, narrow cypress symbolizes eternity and paradise

**Calligraphic decoration**:
- Quranic verses and poetry inscribed on buildings in elaborate scripts
- Often integrated into architectural borders and medallions
- Thuluth, Nastaliq, and Naskh scripts used decoratively
- Verses are sometimes arranged in geometric patterns (calligraphic tessellation)

### Flower Traditions in Muslim Weddings

- **Rose (Gulab)**: The quintessential flower in Muslim wedding culture. Rose water (gulab jal) sprinkled on guests, rose petals showered on the couple, rose garlands for the bride and groom. Deep cultural association with romance, beauty, and paradise (roses are described in Islamic tradition as connected to the Prophet Muhammad, PBUH — needs verification of specific hadith).
- **Jasmine (Mogra / Chameli)**: White, fragrant flowers woven into the bride's hair (gajra). Symbolizes purity, grace, and femininity. The evening-blooming quality makes it associated with the wedding night.
- **Marigold (Genda)**: Used extensively in Indian Muslim weddings for stage decoration, garlands, and venue embellishment. While marigold is more associated with Hindu worship, its use in Muslim weddings is cultural and decorative rather than religious.
- **Tuberose (Rajnigandha)**: Fragrant white flowers used in garlands and stage decoration. Popular in South Indian Muslim weddings.
- **Lotus**: While culturally significant across India, the lotus has stronger Hindu and Buddhist associations. Use carefully — it appears in Mughal art but should not dominate a Muslim wedding template.

### Fabric & Textile References

- **Zari work (gold thread embroidery)**: Heavy gold or silver metallic thread woven or embroidered into fabric. The hallmark of bridal wear — lehnga, sharara, gharara, and sherwanis all feature zari.
- **Zardozi**: Heavy, raised embroidery using gold/silver bullion wire, sequins, beads, and precious stones. Creates a three-dimensional, luxurious surface texture.
- **Chikankari**: Delicate white-on-white embroidery from Lucknow. Subtle, elegant, and associated with Awadhi Muslim culture. Patterns include: jaali (net-like), murri (rice grain), phanda (knot stitch), and bakhia (shadow work).
- **Brocade (Kinkhab/Himru)**: Richly patterned woven fabrics with gold or silver thread, originating from Banaras and Hyderabad. Used in bridal outfits and wedding decorations.
- **Net/Tulle with embroidery**: Modern Muslim bridal dupattas often use net fabric with zari or zardozi borders.
- **Jamawar**: Woven paisley-patterned shawl fabric from Kashmir, used in sherwanis and bridal wraps. Rich, flowing paisley motifs.

### Stage Decoration (Wedding Stage / Jhoola)

The Muslim wedding stage (also called the "mandap" in a cultural context, though it is not a sacred mandap like in Hindu ceremonies) is the visual centerpiece.

**Traditional elements:**
- Raised platform (dias) where the couple sits after the Nikah
- Ornate sofa or throne-like seating (masnad) for the couple
- Draped fabric canopy — often in white, cream, gold, or blush with heavy embellishment
- Flower walls: entire panels of roses, carnations, and hydrangeas as backdrop
- Mogra and rose garland curtains framing the stage
- Crystal or brass chandeliers and hanging lanterns
- Gold-framed mirrors and candelabras
- Modern: LED backdrops, acrylic geometric structures with flowers

**The Jhoola (swing):**
- Some Muslim weddings feature a decorated swing (jhoola) for the bride
- Draped with flowers and fabric
- A romantic, feminine element from Lucknowi/Awadhi culture

## 3. Architecture & Settings

### Mughal Architecture

Mughal architecture is the defining visual language for Indian Muslim weddings. It represents the pinnacle of Indo-Islamic artistic synthesis.

**Key architectural elements:**

- **Pointed/Cusped arches (Mehrab)**: The pointed arch (also called the ogee or pointed horseshoe) is the signature profile. Mughal arches often have a cusped (multi-lobed) interior edge with a smooth pointed exterior. The "Mughal arch" typically has a wider, more bulbous profile than a Gothic pointed arch — it swells outward before tapering to a point.
- **Domes**: Bulbous/onion-shaped domes, often with a lotus-bud finial on top and a decorative drum (cylindrical base). The Taj Mahal dome is the archetype — slightly bulbous with a tapered top, sitting on a high drum.
- **Minarets**: Slender cylindrical towers with cupola tops, flanking mosques and tombs. Usually in pairs or groups of four. Feature multiple balconies (guldasta) and are topped with small domes or chhatris.
- **Jharokha (Overhanging balcony)**: Enclosed projecting window/balcony with a cusped arch and decorative brackets. Used for "darshan" (viewing). Beautiful framing element.
- **Jali screens (Lattice screens)**: Perforated stone or marble screens with geometric patterns. Found in windows, railings, and as room dividers. Create dappled light effects. The Taj Mahal has some of the finest marble jali work.
- **Charbagh (Four-fold garden)**: The Mughal garden layout — a square or rectangular garden divided into four equal quadrants by water channels meeting at a central pool/platform. Represents the four rivers of paradise described in the Quran.
- **Water features**: Reflecting pools, fountains, and water channels are integral to Mughal design. The reflection of architecture in still water is a core aesthetic.
- **Iwans**: Large rectangular recessed entrance portals framing the main doorway. Often decorated with calligraphy and geometric tilework.
- **Chatri (Kiosk/pavilion)**: Small domed, pillared pavilions at the roofline. Found on every Mughal building.
- **Pishtaq (Entrance portal)**: A large, monumental arched portal that projects from the facade. Frames the entrance grandly.

**Specific architectural references:**

- **Taj Mahal (Agra)**: The ultimate symbol of eternal love — built by Emperor Shah Jahan in memory of his wife Mumtaz Mahal. White marble, pietra dura inlay, symmetry, reflecting gardens, river backdrop. The most directly relevant architectural reference for a wedding template.
- **Jama Masjid (Delhi)**: India's largest mosque — red sandstone and white marble. Dramatic arched gateways and open courtyard.
- **Humayun's Tomb (Delhi)**: Precursor to the Taj. Red sandstone with white marble accents, charbagh garden, bulbous dome.
- **Fatehpur Sikri**: Mughal city with the Buland Darwaza (the tallest gateway in the world) — red sandstone with intricate carving.
- **Charminar (Hyderabad)**: Four minarets on four arches — the iconic symbol of Hyderabadi Muslim culture.

### Haveli-style Elements

Havelis (grand merchant houses) of Lucknow, Hyderabad, and North India:

- **Ornate doorways (Darwaza)**: Large, arched wooden doors with brass studs, geometric panels, and carved surrounds
- **Central courtyard (Sahn)**: Open-to-sky central space surrounded by rooms — a key feature of Muslim domestic architecture
- **Carved plaster work (Gach-kari)**: Ornamental plasterwork on ceilings and walls, featuring floral, geometric, and calligraphic motifs
- **Stained glass (Sheesh)**: Colored glass panels set in geometric or floral patterns in windows and transoms
- **Chandelier-lit interiors**: Crystal chandeliers (jhumar) in reception halls and nawabi settings

### Garden/Paradise Settings (Bagh)

- The Quran describes paradise (Jannah) as gardens with flowing water, shade, fruits, and eternal spring
- Mughal gardens (Shalimar Bagh in Kashmir, Charbagh gardens) are earthly interpretations of this paradise
- Elements: symmetrical layout, water channels, fountains, cypress trees, fruit-bearing trees, flower beds in geometric patterns
- Nighttime garden settings with lanterns and candles are common for Nikah and Walima events

## 4. Typography & Calligraphy

### Islamic Calligraphic Traditions

Calligraphy (Khattati/Khat) is considered the highest art form in Islamic culture — the visual embodiment of the divine word. In wedding contexts, it carries deep spiritual and aesthetic significance.

**Major script styles relevant to wedding invitations:**

- **Nastaliq**: The script of Urdu poetry and literature. Flowing, elegant, with a distinctive rightward slant and hanging baseline. Letters are written at an angle, with long, sweeping descenders. This is the quintessential Urdu calligraphy style. Even when text is in English, the flowing, diagonal aesthetic of Nastaliq can influence font selection.
- **Naskh**: The most widely used script for printed Arabic/Urdu text. More regular and upright than Nastaliq. Clean, readable, and dignified. Common for Quranic text.
- **Thuluth**: A large, ornamental script used for architectural inscriptions and chapter headings. Bold, majestic, with elaborate flourishes. The "display font" of Islamic calligraphy.
- **Diwani**: Ornate, dense script developed in the Ottoman court. Very decorative, with letters crowded together and dramatic curves. Used for official documents and certificates — could inspire the Nikahnama section.

## 5. Color Significance & Palette Research

### Traditional Color Associations

**Green (Sabz)**:
- The most important color in Islamic tradition
- Associated with paradise (Jannah), the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH), life, renewal, and nature
- The Prophet's cloak and dome of the Prophet's Mosque in Medina are green
- Green is considered the color of Islam itself
- Range: from deep emerald to bright parrot green to sage
- Hex range: `#006400` (deep) to `#2E8B57` (medium) to `#228B22` (true green)

**Gold (Sunehri)**:
- Represents divine light, prosperity, blessing, and glory
- Omnipresent in Islamic decorative arts — gold leaf in manuscripts, gilded domes, zari textiles
- Gold calligraphy on dark grounds is a classic Islamic manuscript treatment
- Both warm (yellow-gold) and white gold (silver-gold) are traditional
- Hex range: `#C9A84C` (burnished) to `#D4A843` (rich) to `#E8C76A` (light)

**White (Safed)**:
- Represents purity, peace, and simplicity
- The color of the Hajj pilgrim's garment (ihram)
- White marble of the Taj Mahal and many mosques
- Unlike in Hindu tradition, white is NOT a mourning color in Islam — it is positive and auspicious
- Used extensively in Muslim weddings — white decor, white flowers, white outfits for the bride (influenced by Western bridal tradition but also rooted in Islamic preference for white)
- This is a KEY DIFFERENCE from Hindu wedding templates where white is avoided

**Maroon / Deep Red (Mehroon)**:
- Rich, regal, and romantic
- Common in bridal wear — lehenga, sharara, gharara in deep maroon/burgundy
- Associated with love, passion, and festivity
- The interior of many mosques features deep maroon accents
- Hex range: `#6B1D2A` (deep) to `#800020` (burgundy) to `#722F37`

**Red (Surkh / Lal)**:
- Bridal and celebratory
- While not as religiously significant as green, red is culturally dominant in South Asian Muslim weddings
- Red bangles, red bridal outfit, red rose petals
- More orange-reds (vermillion) are associated with Hindu sindoor, so deeper, cooler reds (crimson, burgundy, maroon) are more appropriate for Muslim wedding contexts

**Ivory / Cream (Haathi Daant)**:
- The color of aged marble, parchment, and carved stone
- Warm, sophisticated base color for invitation backgrounds
- Evokes the marble of Mughal architecture
- Hex range: `#F5EDD6` to `#FAF7F0` to `#FFFFF0`

**Royal Blue / Navy (Neela)**:
- Associated with the night sky, depth, and divine mystery
- Blue tilework is prominent in Central Asian Islamic architecture (Samarkand, Isfahan)
- Less dominant in Indian Muslim weddings specifically but used as an accent
- Deep navy is common in groom's wear (sherwani)
- Hex range: `#1A3A6B` to `#0F2D5A` to `#191970`

**Purple / Plum (Baingani)**:
- Historically associated with royalty and luxury
- Appears in Mughal miniature paintings and textiles
- A secondary accent color, not a primary choice

**Pink / Blush (Gulabi)**:
- Modern Indian Muslim weddings increasingly use soft pink and blush tones
- Influenced by contemporary wedding trends
- Pastel pinks evoke a softer, modern romance aesthetic
- Popular for mehendi outfits and modern bridal wear

### Colors to AVOID

- **Saffron/Orange (Bhagwa)**: While not inherently problematic, saffron/deep orange has strong Hindu religious associations (saffron robes, saffron flag in Hindu nationalism). In the current Indian political climate, it can be perceived as communally coded. Avoid as a dominant color. Small amounts in marigold flowers or as warm gold undertones are acceptable.
- **Black as dominant**: Black is considered inauspicious for weddings in most South Asian traditions (Muslim included). Small black accents (text, outlines) are fine, but a black-dominated palette is inappropriate.
- **Neon or fluorescent colors**: Inauthentic and disrespectful to the traditional aesthetic.
- **Pure, cold white (#FFFFFF)**: While white is not negative in Islam, stark cold white feels clinical. Use warm whites and ivories.

### Color Principles for This Template

1. **Gold is the sacred thread** — it connects to Islamic decorative arts across all palettes. Always present in borders, calligraphy accents, and ornamental elements.

## 6. Music & Sound Traditions

### Traditional Instruments

- **Shehnai**: The auspicious double-reed wind instrument played at the start of the Nikah, during the baraat, and at key ceremonial moments. The most iconic sound of an Indian wedding.
- **Dholak**: A two-headed hand drum. Fundamental to mehendi night celebrations. Women play dholak and sing traditional wedding songs (geet). Its rhythmic, intimate sound is the heartbeat of pre-wedding celebrations.
- **Tabla**: Pair of hand drums (one large, one small) played with the fingers. More refined than the dholak. Used in classical and semi-classical musical performances.
- **Dhol**: Large barrel drum played with sticks, associated with Baraat processions. Energetic, rhythmic, and loud — the sound of the groom's procession.
- **Sitar**: Stringed instrument associated with classical music. Used in more refined, Lucknowi-style wedding settings.
- **Harmonium**: A hand-pumped keyboard instrument. Accompanies Qawwali singing and is used in mehendi songs.

### Qawwali

- Sufi devotional music form, deeply associated with Indian Muslim culture
- Originated in the Chishti Sufi tradition in the Indian subcontinent
- Made world-famous by Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan and his successors
- Features a lead singer (Qawwal) with a group of supporting singers and hand-clapping
- Themes: divine love, spiritual ecstasy, and romantic love (often ambiguous, blending earthly and divine)
- Qawwali nights are a hallmark of elaborate Indian Muslim weddings
- Classic wedding Qawwali songs: "Aaj Rang Hai", "Bhar Do Jholi Meri", "Tumhe Dillagi Bhool Jani Padegi"

### Nasheeds (Vocal Hymns)

- Islamic vocal music, traditionally without instruments (though modern nasheeds may include percussion)
- Some families prefer nasheeds over Bollywood music for religious reasons
- Wedding nasheeds celebrate the beauty of the marital union as blessed by Allah
- Sami Yusuf, Maher Zain, and other contemporary nasheed artists are popular at weddings

### Modern Music at Indian Muslim Weddings

- Bollywood songs dominate at Sangeet and Baraat (Bollywood culture is pan-Indian, not religion-specific)
- DJ music is common at the baraat and reception
- Some families maintain a more traditional approach with Qawwali and mehendi geet only
- The invitation template should evoke the auspicious sound of the shehnai rather than Bollywood energy

## 7. Cultural Sensitivities & Respectful Representation

### Representational Guidelines

1. **No depiction of human faces or full figures**: Many interpretations of Islamic art avoid figurative representation. While Indian Muslim culture is more relaxed about this than Middle Eastern traditions, the safest approach for a template is to avoid illustrated human faces. Use silhouettes, abstract couple outlines, or focus on decorative/architectural elements instead.

2. **No depiction of religious figures**: Never depict the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH), angels, or any prophets. This is a firm prohibition in Islam.

3. **Modesty in representation**: If couples or figures are suggested (silhouettes, abstract forms), they should be modestly dressed. No exposed skin beyond hands and face. Bridal depictions should show dupatta/hijab coverage.

4. **Bismillah is a requirement, not decoration**: The Bismillah ("In the name of Allah...") at the top of the invitation is not merely decorative — it is a spiritual necessity for many families. It should be given prominent, respectful placement, typically at the very top of the invitation, before any other content.

5. **Quranic verses**: If Quranic verses are included, they must be accurate. Misquoted or incorrectly written Arabic text is deeply disrespectful. Use verified text or well-known verses only.

6. **Right-to-left text**: Arabic and Urdu text reads right to left. Ensure any Arabic/Urdu elements are correctly oriented. Mirrored or upside-down Arabic calligraphy is a common and offensive error.

7. **Alcohol references**: Do not include imagery of champagne glasses, wine bottles, or cocktails. Many Muslim weddings are alcohol-free, and such imagery would be inappropriate.

8. **Cross-religious sensitivity**: Avoid Hindu religious symbols (Om, swastika, tilak, sindoor) in a Muslim wedding template. While cultural elements (mehendi, marigold, baraat) are shared, religious symbols are specific. Also avoid the crescent-and-star combined symbol, which is a national/political symbol (Ottoman/Turkish/Pakistani flag) rather than an inherently Islamic religious one — some Indian Muslims may find it politically charged.

9. **Gender separation awareness**: In traditional Muslim weddings, men and women may celebrate separately. The template should not assume co-ed settings for all events.

10. **Halal food reference**: If any food imagery or mentions are included (Walima section), be aware that all food at Muslim weddings is halal. Avoid any pork or non-halal food imagery.

### Things That Would Be Considered Inappropriate or Offensive

- Using Arabic calligraphy purely as decoration without caring whether the text means anything (or means something inappropriate)
- Depicting the couple touching or in intimate poses (a modest side-by-side or hands-only approach is preferred)
- Using temple or mandir imagery in a Muslim wedding template
- Confusing Sikh, Hindu, and Muslim wedding elements (they share some cultural elements but not religious ones)
- Using a pig or dog motif in any decorative element (both are culturally sensitive in Islamic contexts)
- Placing Quranic text near the ground, at the bottom of the page, or near feet — sacred text should always be elevated
- Playing music over Quranic recitation in any audio context

### What Makes This Template Distinctly "Indian Muslim" vs. Generic Islamic

Indian Muslim weddings are a beautiful synthesis of Islamic faith and Indian cultural exuberance. The template should capture both:

- **Islamic elements**: Bismillah, geometric patterns, calligraphy, green, Quranic blessings, modest representation
- **Indian elements**: Mehendi, vibrant colors, marigold garlands, baraat, dholak/shehnai, lehnga/sharara bridal wear, elaborate food tradition
- **The synthesis**: Mughal architecture (which IS Indo-Islamic), Lucknowi/Hyderabadi cultural refinement, Qawwali (which is uniquely subcontinental Sufi), the specific visual vocabulary of Indian Muslim decorative arts

This is NOT a Middle Eastern wedding template. It is not an Arab wedding. It is not a Turkish wedding. It is specifically Indian Muslim — which means it draws from the Mughal aesthetic, Lucknowi/Awadhi elegance, Hyderabadi grandeur, and the shared pan-Indian celebration culture.
