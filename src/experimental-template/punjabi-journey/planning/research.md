# Punjabi Journey — Cultural Research Brief

Scope: Punjabi wedding (Sikh and Hindu Punjabi), village / haveli / pind flavour, for a scroll-driven invitation where the guest walks through the story.

**Confidence key**
- **[G]** historically grounded, well-documented practice
- **[M]** modern interpretation, widely practised today
- **[D]** decorative choice, fits visually but isn't specifically Punjabi
- **[?]** uncertain. Check with a Punjabi family member or granthi before you ship it.

This brief comes from established ethnographic knowledge of Punjabi wedding custom. No live web sources were consulted. Gurmukhi spellings are given only where they are standard. Families vary a lot, so treat the sequence as typical rather than fixed.

---

## 0. Two branches, one region

| | Sikh (Anand Karaj) | Hindu Punjabi (Phere) |
|---|---|---|
| Core rite | ਲਾਵਾਂ *Laavan*: four circlings around Guru Granth Sahib in a gurdwara | Pheras around the sacred fire (*agni*) under a *mandap/vedi* |
| Time | **Morning**, traditionally finished before noon **[G]** | Evening / night, at a *muhurat* set by the pandit **[G]** |
| Signals | Palla, raagi kirtan, karah prashad, Ardas | Sindoor, mangalsutra (adopted later), havan smoke, *kanyadaan* |
| Do not mix | No fire, sindoor or mandap in a Sikh ceremony | No Guru Granth Sahib in a Hindu ceremony |

Everything before and after the core rite (vatna, jaago, mehndi, chooda, ghodi, milni, doli) is **shared Punjabi folk custom**. The template should be built on that shared layer, with the core rite as a configurable slot.

Season: Punjabi weddings cluster in **November–February**. That gives you winter fog, shawls, sarson da saag, bonfire light, and mustard in bloom (mostly Jan–Feb) **[G]**.

## 1. Canonical sequence

### 1. Roka / Thaka → Kurmai / Sagan, Chunni Chadhana
- **What:** The families formally agree to the match. The bride's side visits the groom (*sagan*): tilak in Hindu families, gifts, and the groom is fed *chhuare* (dried dates). In Sikh families this is the **Kurmai** ਕੁੜਮਾਈ, done with an Ardas, often at a gurdwara or at home. Later the groom's women bring a red **chunni** and place it over the bride's head (*chunni chadhana*) **[G]**.
- **Light:** Daytime, indoors, in the baithak or front room.
- **Who:** Elders from both families, parents, a few close relatives.
- **Objects:** Chhuare, a shagun thaal (tray with dry fruit, mithai, cash in a red envelope), red chunni, a coconut (Hindu), kara (gifted to the groom in some Sikh families **[?]**).
- **Sounds:** Quiet talk, an Ardas or blessing, laughter. No dhol yet.
- **Colour:** Red chunni against plain lime-wash walls.
- **Emotion:** The word is given. This is the private beginning.

### 2. Maiyan & Vatna ਮਾਈਆਂ · ਵਟਣਾ
- **What:** Over several mornings before the wedding, the bride (and separately the groom, at his house) sits on a low **peerhi** ਪੀੜ੍ਹੀ stool in old clothes. Women rub on **vatna**, a paste of haldi, besan, mustard oil and rosewater. Four girls or married women hold a red or phulkari cloth canopy over the bride. During *maiyan* she traditionally doesn't leave the house **[G]**. Groom's side: on the last day his bhabhi brings water in a decorated pot, the **gharoli**, often from a gurdwara or well, for his ritual bath **[G]**, with the water source varying by family **[?]**.
- **Light:** Morning sun in the courtyard (vehra).
- **Who:** Women of the household and neighbourhood.
- **Objects:** Peerhi, a brass bowl of yellow vatna, a phulkari canopy held at four corners, a lota, the gharoli pot with mauli thread tied round it.
- **Sounds:** *Suhaag* songs (bride's side) and *ghodiyan* (groom's side) **[G]**. Dholki.
- **Colour:** Turmeric yellow on skin and cloth, against an old faded suit.
- **Food:** Panjiri, laddoo for the singers.

### 3. Mehndi ਮਹਿੰਦੀ
- **What:** Henna on the bride's hands and feet. The groom's name or initials are hidden in the design, and he has to find them later **[M]**, a widespread custom whose exact origin is unclear. Mehndi also comes from the groom's side as a gift **[G]**. Women dance *giddha* and trade *boliyan* (teasing rhyming couplets) **[G]**.
- **Light:** Afternoon running into evening, under string lights.
- **Who:** Women. Men stay at the edges.
- **Objects:** Mehndi cones on a tray, a cushion or charpai for the bride, a lemon-sugar bowl (daubed on to deepen the stain), a parandi (hair tassel), a dholki.
- **Sounds:** Dholki, the **chimta** (tong idiophone with jingles) **[G]**, clapping, boliyan.
- **Colour:** Wet henna olive-green that dries to orange-maroon; bright suits.

### 4. Jaago ਜਾਗੋ: the loudest night
- **What:** The night before the wedding (sometimes two nights before), the bride's **naanke** (maternal family) arrive. A woman carries on her head a brass or copper **gaagar/gadva** fitted with burning dough or clay diyas. The women parade through the village lanes, knocking on doors, teasing neighbours, demanding treats and dancing, while someone shakes a stick hung with bells **[G]** (the stick's name varies by area **[?]**). The call is *"Jaago aayi aa"* ("the Jaago has come") **[G]**.
- **Light:** Full night. Flickering oil flame on brass is the only warm light in dark lanes.
- **Who:** Naanke, village women, children running ahead. Men follow, and on the night some of them keep a respectful distance.
- **Objects:** Lamp-lit gaagar, belled stick, dholki, shawls, a basket of gur/patashe given out at doorways.
- **Sounds:** Bells, dholki, boliyan shouted lane to lane, dogs barking, doors banging open, laughter.
- **Colour:** Black night, amber flame, brass glints, bright dupattas moving through fog.

### 5. Chooda & Kalire ਚੂੜਾ · ਕਲੀਰੇ (wedding-day dawn)
- **What:** The bride's **mama** (mother's brother) brings the **chooda**, a set of red and ivory/white bangles (ivory historically, acrylic today). It soaks overnight in *kachi lassi* (milk and water). A havan or Ardas may come first. The mama slips it on while the bride's eyes are covered or turned away, because she's not meant to see it until she is dressed **[G]**. Sisters and friends then tie on the **kalire**: dangling gold-tone umbrella or dome ornaments. The bride shakes them over the heads of unmarried girls, and whoever a piece falls on is said to marry next **[G]**.
- **Light:** Pre-dawn or early morning, cool blue light turning gold. Fits the Sikh morning-ceremony timetable.
- **Who:** Mama and mami, mother, sisters, girl cousins.
- **Objects:** Chooda in a red box or wrapped in cloth, a steel bowl of milk-lassi, kalire, a red chunni, the bride's bare wrists.
- **Sounds:** Hushed. Suhaag songs, tears, the clink of bangles.
- **Colour:** **Red and ivory**, the single most recognisable Punjabi bridal signifier.

### 6. Sehrabandi & Ghodi Chadhna ਸਿਹਰਾ · ਘੋੜੀ (groom's house, morning)
- **What:** A family elder, sister or jija ties the **sehra** (a veil of flowers or pearl strings) over the groom's turban, often with a *kalgi* (crest) **[G]**. His bhabhi lines his eyes with **surma** and is paid for it **[G]**. A small boy, the **sarbala** (a nephew or young cousin), is dressed like a miniature groom and stays at his side **[G]**. The groom mounts a decorated mare (**ghodi**). His sisters feed her soaked chana daal and hold her reins until he pays them: **vaag pharai** **[G]**. Many Sikh grooms now go by car and keep the ghodi only for a short stretch **[M]**.
- **Light:** Morning.
- **Objects:** Sehra, kalgi, surma-dani, the mare's embroidered saddle-cloth and tassels, a bowl of chana daal, reins wrapped in red thread.
- **Sounds:** Sisters singing *ghodiyan* **[G]**, dhol starting up.

### 7. Baraat ਬਰਾਤ
- **What:** The groom's party travels to the bride's village or gurdwara, dancing bhangra behind the dhol. Notes are waved over the groom and handed to the dholi or poor children (*vel*/*vaarna*) **[G/?]**.
- **Light:** Morning for Sikh weddings; evening for Hindu baraats, often with a band and lights.
- **Objects:** **Dhol** and its curved stick (*dagga*), currency garlands, turbans (often matching pink or saffron for the groom's side **[M]**), a decorated car or ghodi.
- **Sounds:** Dhol rhythm (bhangra *chaal*), *"Balle balle!"*, *"Hoye hoye!"* **[G]**, car horns.
- **Colour:** Matching turbans in a single hue, marigold, dust.

### 8. Milni ਮਿਲਣੀ
- **What:** At the bride's gate, the two families meet in matched pairs (fathers, then mamas, then chachas and so on). Each pair embraces and swaps a shawl/kambal and a shagun envelope. Sikh milnis start with an Ardas **[G]**. Playful lifting contests are a modern addition **[M]**. Then breakfast: chai, pakore, jalebi, matthi.
- **Light:** Morning (Sikh) or evening (Hindu).
- **Objects:** Folded shawls, marigold garlands, shagun envelopes, a list of who meets whom.
- **Sounds:** Shabad or Ardas, then the noise breaks loose. A raagi or announcer calls each pair.
- **Emotion:** Two families visibly becoming one.

### 9a. Anand Karaj ਅਨੰਦ ਕਾਰਜ (Sikh)
- **What:** In the gurdwara darbar, the couple sit before Guru Granth Sahib. The bride's father places one end of the groom's **palla** (scarf) in her hand (*palla pharana*) **[G]**. Raagis sing the four **Laavan** (composed by Guru Ram Das, in Raag Suhi) **[G]**. At each lav the couple walk **clockwise** around the Guru Granth Sahib, groom leading **[G]**. Ardas follows, then karah prashad, then langar.
- **Light:** Morning daylight through windows; white sheets on the floor; everyone seated cross-legged, heads covered.
- **Objects that are safe to depict:** The palla (scarf), the couple's backs and joined hands, covered heads, rows of shoes left at the *joda ghar*, a steel langar thaali, a chunni.
- **Sounds:** Harmonium and tabla kirtan, then silence. This is the quietest moment of the wedding.
- **Colour:** Bride in red, maroon or pink, groom in an achkan with a turban that often matches his side; plain white floor.

### 9b. Phere (Hindu Punjabi)
- **What:** Under the mandap, *kanyadaan*, the tying of the knot (*gathbandhan*), the circlings of the fire (*phere*; four or seven depending on family, often 4+3 **[?]**), sindoor and mangalsutra. *Joota chupai*: the bride's sisters steal the groom's shoes and ransom them back **[M/G]**. This happens in both traditions.
- **Light:** Night, by firelight.

### 10. Doli / Vidaai ਡੋਲੀ: the farewell
- **What:** In the afternoon, the bride leaves her parents' house. Walking out of the gate, she throws handfuls of **phulian** (puffed rice) back over her head without turning round, a blessing so the house stays full **[G]**. Brothers and cousins push the car (historically a palanquin, the *doli*) a short way. Coins are thrown over it for children **[G]**. Everyone cries.
- **Song:** *"Saada chiṛiyan da chamba ve, babul asaan uḍ jaana"* ("We are a flock of sparrows, father; we will fly away"). This is the classic Punjabi vidaai folk song **[G]**.
- **Light:** Low winter afternoon sun, long shadows across the courtyard and gate.
- **Objects:** Phulian in a thaal, kalire swinging, a phulkari or chunni over her head, the gate, the car or doli, the daaj trunk being loaded.

### 11. Arrival at the groom's house: Pani Vaarna & games
- **What:** At the threshold the groom's mother circles a glass or lota of water over the couple's heads seven times and tries to drink it, and the groom stops her (*pani vaarna*) **[G]**. Mustard oil is poured on the doorposts **[G]**. Then games: digging for a ring in a bowl of milk or water, and untying the *gana* (wrist thread) **[G]**. The bride's mehndi name is searched for.
- **Light:** Evening, lamps.
- **Emotion:** Release, laughter, welcome.

### 12. Phera Pauna & Reception
- **Phera pauna:** The next day the bride goes back to her parents' house with the groom for a visit **[G]**.
- **Reception:** An urban/modern event at a hall or tent at the groom's end **[M]**, with DJ, buffet and stage. Fine to include as the "information" stop, but it is not folk tradition.

## 2. Emotional curve

```
NOISE   ─────────────╮Jaago           ╭Baraat               ╭Homecoming games
                     │               │                      │
TENDER  ──Roka──Vatna─┴──Mehndi──Chooda┘─Milni─╮              │
                                             │              │
HUSH                                         ╰Laavan/Phere   │
FAREWELL                                           ╰──Doli───┘
```

The strongest beats are, in order:

1. **Doli (farewell).** This is the peak, the one every Punjabi recognises. The phulian thrown backward, the song, brothers pushing the car. Give it the most scroll length and the slowest pacing.
2. **Jaago (noise and joy).** The only ritual that is pure night-time collective chaos, which makes it the natural set-piece for motion and light.
3. **Chooda at dawn (tenderness).** The mama's hands, the covered eyes, the milk bowl. An intimate close-up.
4. **Laavan (hush).** The volume drops to almost nothing. That contrast is the point, and it must be handled respectfully (see section 5).
5. **Pani vaarna (homecoming).** Laughter after tears. It ends the story on warmth instead of grief.

The roka/chunni beat holds *anticipation*: quiet and private, a good opener.

## 3. Spatial settings

| Setting | Punjabi term | Ritual | What the viewer sees |
|---|---|---|---|
| Haveli gateway | **ਡਿਓੜੀ** *deorhi* | Milni, Doli, arrival | A heavy carved wooden double door with iron studs, set in a thin-brick (*nanakshahi* brick) arch; marigold strings; a brass name plate; a pair of earthen *ghara* or brass pots beside it |
| Courtyard | **ਵਿਹੜਾ** *vehra* | Vatna, Chooda, Mehndi | Lime-washed or mud-plastered walls, a peerhi, a *nalka* (hand pump), a neem or tahli tree, a clay chulha, and charpais stacked against the wall after being borrowed from neighbours **[G]** |
| Rooftop | **ਕੋਠਾ** *kotha* | Jaago (watching the lane), night | Flat roof, low parapet, a loudspeaker horn tied to a pole (classic village-wedding signal **[M]**), string lights (*lareeyan*), fog over neighbouring roofs, distant tube-well lights |
| Men's sitting room | ਬੈਠਕ *baithak* | Roka/Kurmai, Milni prep | Takhtposh, bolsters, a hookah (avoid in Sikh households, see section 5), a wall clock, framed elders' photos, a steel glass of lassi |
| Village lane | *gali* | Jaago, Baraat | Narrow brick lanes, open doorways, women on the doorsteps, buffaloes tethered, a dung-cake wall |
| Mustard field | ਸਰ੍ਹੋਂ *sarhon* | Baraat road, transitions | Flat yellow to the horizon, a kikar tree, a tube-well *koti* (pump hut), a tractor on the field path, winter fog |
| Gurdwara | | Anand Karaj, Kurmai | **Exterior only:** white walls, the *joda ghar* shoe rows, a sarovar edge or water tap for washing feet, a saffron Nishan Sahib seen **at a distance** |
| Tent/shamiana | | Mehndi, reception | A patterned shamiana with red, yellow and green panels, a *halwai bhatti* (cauldron on a fire) frying jalebi, rented chairs |

The haveli should be **Punjabi brick vernacular**: modest, warm and lived-in. Not Rajasthani sandstone, not a Mughal palace.

## 4. Objects that carry invitation information

| # | Object | Ritual | Information | How it works in the scene |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Chhuara + shagun envelope** | Roka | Couple names, "save the date" | A red envelope on a shagun thaal; open it to reveal names |
| 2 | **Red chunni** | Chunni chadhana | Bride and groom names and families | Gold gota border stitching spells the names as it settles over a head |
| 3 | **Phulkari / bagh** | Maiyan canopy, daaj | **Full event schedule** | Each embroidered geometric band is one event; a darning-stitch row gives the time. Grandmothers stitched a bagh for a girl's wedding **[G]** |
| 4 | **Jaago gaagar lamps** | Jaago | **Countdown** | The number of diyas lit on the pot = days remaining; the flames grow as the date nears |
| 5 | **Dholki / dhol skin** | Mehndi, Baraat | Sangeet/mehndi time; baraat assembly time and place | Text painted on the drum head; tapping it plays a beat (audio toggle) |
| 6 | **Mehndi palm** | Mehndi | **Wedding date**, the hidden groom's name | Date drawn into the henna pattern; "find the name" interaction |
| 7 | **Chooda box** | Chooda | Wedding date and morning-ceremony time | A red velvet box lid opens to reveal the chooda resting in milk |
| 8 | **Kalire** | Chooda | **RSVP** | Shake the kalire; one falls on you and says "You're next — confirm you'll come" |
| 9 | **Sehra strands** | Sehrabandi | Groom's-side timings | Each pearl or flower string ends in a tiny tag (sehrabandi, ghodi, departure) |
| 10 | **Ghodi reins (vaag)** | Vaag pharai | Baraat departure time | A red-thread tassel on the reins carries a tag |
| 11 | **Milni shawl + list** | Milni | Family introductions ("who meets whom"), hosts | Folded shawls stacked in pairs, each with a name tag |
| 12 | **Joda ghar token** | Anand Karaj | **Gurdwara address / map link** | A numbered metal shoe token. An everyday object, safe and respectful |
| 13 | **Borrowed charpai** | Courtyard prep | **Guest seat / RSVP confirmation** | "A manja is kept for you", stencilled with the guest's name **[G practice of borrowing manje]** |
| 14 | **Daaj sandook (trunk)** | Doli | Dress code, what to wear, gift note | Brass-cornered wooden trunk; lifting the lid shows folded suits and a note |
| 15 | **Phulian thaal** | Doli | A thank-you from the family | Puffed rice scatters to reveal the message |
| 16 | **Pani vaarna lota** | Homecoming | Reception venue and time | Engraved brass lota |
| 17 | **Deorhi brass plate** | Gateway | "Hosted by [family], [village]" | The house name plate on the haveli door |
| 18 | **Kotha loudspeaker** | Village signal | Music on/off, announcements | The horn speaker on the rooftop is the audio control |
| 19 | **Mithai/laddoo box** | Shagun, reception | Menu and food timings | A cardboard mithai box with a printed top |

Strongest picks: 3 (phulkari schedule), 4 (jaago countdown), 6 (mehndi date), 8 (kalire RSVP) and 12 (joda ghar location). Each comes straight from ritual logic, not decoration.

## 5. Things to avoid

### Sacred imagery: hard rules
- **Never depict Guru Granth Sahib**, the palki (canopy) it sits under, the chaur (whisk), or the darbar interior with the scripture. Show the Anand Karaj through the palla, joined hands, covered heads and backs, or simply through sound and light.
- **Never depict the Sikh Gurus, Hindu deities, or any religious figure**, whether as illustration, silhouette or icon.
- **Khanda:** don't use it as a decorative motif, logo, divider or pattern. If a Nishan Sahib appears, keep it small and distant, with no detailed emblem.
- **Gurbani text:** don't use Laavan or other scriptural verses as decorative typography or animate them. If the couple wants a line, use it only with family approval and keep it static.
- **Gurdwara:** exterior only. No alcohol, meat, tobacco, uncovered heads or shoes in any gurdwara-adjacent scene. In 2021 the Akal Takht directed that Anand Karaj not be held at resorts or banquet venues **[?, verify current wording]**, so don't show an Anand Karaj on a beach or lawn.
- **Hookah, liquor and cigarettes:** never anywhere in a Sikh-branch scene. Use them sparingly or not at all for Hindu Punjabi.
- Karah prashad is sacred. Don't turn it into a cute food icon.

### Mixing traditions within Punjab
- No sindoor, fire, mandap or mangalsutra in a Sikh flow. No Guru Granth Sahib in a Hindu flow.
- Don't show a kirpan unless the family asks for it.
- Giddha is women's dance; bhangra was traditionally men's (now mixed). Jaago is the naanke women's ritual.
- Lohri is a January festival, not a wedding ritual. It can appear only as seasonal flavour, for example a winter bonfire.

### Regional mix-ups
- **Rajasthan:** pink and yellow sandstone palaces, heavy jharokha façades, camels, desert dunes, puppets, ghoomar, bandhani or leheriya, peacocks everywhere.
- **Bengal:** *shakha-pola* (Bengali red and white bangles) is **not** the chooda. Keep the chooda's ivory-and-red stacked look with kalire.
- **South India:** banana-leaf arches, kolam, jasmine strings in the hair, nadaswaram, temple gopurams.
- **Gujarat:** garba or dandiya at the sangeet.
- **Mughal/Bollywood:** Taj-style domes, marble palaces, chandeliers, a generic Bollywood sangeet stage, "Mehndi Laga Ke Rakhna" energy.
- **Caricature:** turban and beard as comedy, cartoon "Balle balle" uncles, tractor-and-gun "jatt" tropes.

### Lesser clichés
- Overusing saffron/kesri as the "wedding colour". Kesri carries religious weight in Sikh contexts.
- Marigold everything. Use it on gates and garlands, not as wallpaper.
- Phulkari as random clip-art texture. Phulkari is geometric darning stitch in golden-yellow silk (*pat*) on khaddar, in red, maroon or rust. Keep the geometry true.

## 6. Journey spines

**A. "One Haveli, One Week": follow the house**
Deorhi (roka) → vehra (vatna, mehndi) → kotha (jaago night) → vehra at dawn (chooda) → gurdwara exterior or mandap → deorhi again (doli, phulian over the shoulder).
- *Pro:* One coherent, buildable space; the gate bookends the story, and the opening door becomes the closing farewell.
- *Con:* The bride's side dominates. The groom's sehra, ghodi and baraat become off-stage sound.

**B. "Dusk to Dusk": one night and one day**
Jaago lanes at night → rooftop fog before dawn → chooda at first light → baraat on the mustard-field road at morning → Laavan or phere (the hush) → doli in afternoon gold → pani vaarna by evening lamps.
- *Pro:* The light arc does the storytelling (amber flame → blue dawn → yellow morning → white hush → long gold → warm lamp) and maps straight onto scroll progress. It fits the Sikh morning timetable naturally.
- *Con:* It compresses rituals that really span several days into one day. Treat that as a poetic frame, and keep roka and mehndi as a short prologue.

**C. "Two Houses, One Road": converging paths**
Alternate the bride's house (vatna, chooda) with the groom's house (sehra, ghodi). The baraat road through the mustard fields brings them together at the **milni** at the gate, then the ceremony, then the doli reverses the road.
- *Pro:* The strongest narrative tension, with both families equal and milni as the payoff.
- *Con:* Twice the assets and settings, and back-and-forth cutting is harder to read in vertical mobile scroll.

**Recommendation:** Use **B as the spine, set inside A's haveli**. One house, one cycle of light, with the baraat arriving through the fields as the single excursion. Doli gets the longest scroll section, and the journey ends at pani vaarna so it closes on welcome, not loss.

## 8. Sources & verification

- Synthesised from documented Punjabi folk custom: suhaag/ghodiyan song traditions, the bagh/phulkari daaj tradition, and Sikh Rehat Maryada guidance on Anand Karaj (morning timing, the four Laavan by Guru Ram Das, circling Guru Granth Sahib).
- **Verify before shipping:** every Gurmukhi string; the name of the jaago bell-stick; the vel/vaarna terminology; the phere count in the Hindu branch; the current wording of the Akal Takht venue directive; whether a kara is gifted at kurmai.
- Strongly recommended: one review pass by a Punjabi Sikh family member and, for Anand Karaj depiction, a granthi or gurdwara committee member.
